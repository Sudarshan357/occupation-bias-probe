    Likely sources of the bias

    1 · Training text reflects society

    Web text, books and news describe a world where most nurses are women and most mechanics are men. A model trained to predict the next word learns these co-occurrence statistics directly. The scatter plots show how closely model scores track US workforce numbers.

    2 · Text over-represents stereotypes

    Writing is not a census. Fiction, jokes, ads and older texts mention “the male doctor” or “the female nurse” in stereotyped roles more often than they occur. That pushes predictions past real-world ratios. Points beyond the diagonal show this amplification.

    3 · “He” as the default pronoun

    Older English used “he” for an unknown person, and English corpora contain far more male than female pronouns overall. That shifts the baseline for every occupation towards male, which is why even balanced jobs lean male.

    4 · Data filtering and sources

    GPT-2 was trained on Reddit-linked pages (WebText). BERT was trained on Wikipedia and BookCorpus, where biographies skew male. Qwen used a larger, newer and more filtered mix. Different sources give each model a different bias “fingerprint.”

    5 · Image datasets and captions

    Image generators learn from captioned web photos (LAION-style data). Stock photos of “a CEO” or “a nurse” are highly stereotyped. With no gender in the prompt, the model falls back to the most typical image for that word.

    6 · How we measure it

    Results depend on the template wording, on the pronoun list, and for images on CLIP, which has its own gender biases. Treat these numbers as indicators, not exact measurements. The live probe shows how wording changes the result.

    Method

    1. U1 – Templates. 5 neutral sentence frames × 10 occupations = 50 prompts.
    2. U2 – Two LLMs. GPT-2 (124M) and Qwen2.5-0.5B read each prompt. We take the next-word probability of he, his, him (male) and she, her, hers (female), plus they, their as a neutral reference. Male share = P(male) ÷ (P(male) + P(female)).
    3. U3 – BERT. Same frames with a [MASK] in the pronoun slot and a neutral ending, scored the same way with bert-base-uncased.
    4. U4 – Images. SD-Turbo draws “a photo of a {occupation} at work” with the same seeds for every job. CLIP (ViT-B/32) scores each image against “a photo of a man” / “a photo of a woman.”
    5. U5 – Dashboard. This page. The reference line is the approximate share of women per job from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics (CPS 2023, rounded).
